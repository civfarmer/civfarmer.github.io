1 · The call

Help with the deck, and an introduction to a fund

The founder wants an introduction to a venture fund and help with the deck. The fund has offered USD 3.4 million for 55 per cent of the holding company, with a term sheet that expires in three weeks; the deck promises “a system efficiency of 81 per cent”.

The clock
3 weeks
on the term sheet; the cooperative’s board meets in six to consider the supply agreement
The decision-makers
Two
the founder, and the partner who holds most of the money and, as a British citizen, most of the legal exposure
Access
4 files
a financial model, a draft supply agreement, a signed lease, a permitting file
Kind of situation
Start-up
no fire, a deadline, a structure to build from nothing

Diagnosis

The decisive constraint

The founder is describing an infrastructure asset as a start-up.

Twenty years of contracted cash flow against a known cost is a debt instrument waiting for a lender. The fund is pricing it as a venture, with venture discounts for execution risk, and asking for the equity those discounts imply.

The three risks

  1. The technical claim failing diligence.
  2. The capital structure diluting the founders out of their own project.
  3. An integrity exposure in the project’s relationships stopping institutional money before either of the first two matters.

Definition of done

A financing structure under which the founders keep control, a lender can lend, and the integrity exposure is managed before money moves.

Agreed after some resistance.

Borrowed urgency

The term sheet’s three weeks were setting the pace of decisions that deserved three months.

The founder’s resistance

He likes the fund. It is fast, its partners are charming, and it does not ask about typhoons. The operator names the resistance rather than arguing with it: “the fund is selling you speed, and the price is control.”

The structure that was built

Ownership and the main contracts. The project company is four-fifths owned by the holding company and one-fifth by a provincial investor; a domestic bank lends in pesos, the currency of the revenue. Solid arrows are ownership and lending; the dashed arrow is the supply agreement.
Financing, in sequence

1. A bridge from the partners for development. 2. A peso loan from a domestic bank for about seventy per cent of the phase-one cost, sized at 1.30 times cover on the P50 case. 3. Refinancing into term project debt once the plant has a year of operation behind it.

Phase two, redrawn

At 3 MW the supply agreement falls inside the 2025 exemption and goes straight to the regulator. Adding 9 MW later “under the same contract” would take the cooperative over the ten-megawatt line, so phase two is redrawn around the competitive process or the right-of-first-refusal route.

3 · The technical claim

Three numbers behind “81 per cent”

The “81 per cent” turns out to be the performance ratio, not an efficiency. The capacity factor in the founder’s own model is what a lender will ask for, and the Global Solar Atlas value for the site supports it.

Modules
3.75MWp

Inverters
3MW
AC rating
Yield
approx. 5,600MWh
a year, from the founder’s model
Capacity factor implied by the model: about 17 per cent on the DC rating and 21 on the AC. The same plant, two honest numbers, neither of them 81.

What the lender’s adviser asked for

  • The site’s typhoon design basis, which the first EPC design did not have. The racking is redesigned, at about four per cent of capital cost.

The discipline the case applies

  • Three numbers, not one, for any technical claim: the module efficiency, the performance ratio and the capacity factor, each quoted with its source. Separate the physical efficiency, the system losses and the utilisation.
  • The risk table before the model. For every risk: who bears it, through which contract or instrument, what is left over, and what the lender will ask.
Two ratios that are not the same thing

A performance ratio says how much of the energy the sunlight on the modules could have produced the plant actually delivered. A capacity factor says how much of the year the plant was, in effect, running at full rating. A lender sizes debt on the second; the deck quoted the first.

4 · What went wrong, and what the method did not settle

The lease, and the land next door

What went wrong

The founder had already signed a lease, on the site the project would eventually use: for ninety-nine years, at a premium, because a friend had told him the longest lease was the safest.

The bank’s counsel did not want ninety-nine years. It wanted the right to take the lease as security, to step in, and to assign it on enforcement, none of which the signed lease allowed. Renegotiating with the landowner took two months and a fee, and the landowner, now aware that the lease mattered, priced the change accordingly.

The lesson: size the lease to the need, and put the lender’s requirements into the draft before anyone signs it.

What a lender wants from a lease

  • To take it as security
  • To step in
  • To assign it on enforcement
The dilemma the method did not settle

Site diligence turned up better-placed land nearby, about three per cent more yield, and integrity diligence produced the finding every provincial project should expect to look for: the family that owned it included the spouse of a member of the cooperative’s board, which would be asked to approve the supply agreement.

Two lawful courses were open. Switch to the better land, disclosing the relationship to the cooperative and the lender, with the board member recused and the land priced on an independent valuation, which the lender said it would accept. Or stay on the land already leased, with the lower yield.

The founders stayed. The method made the choice explicit, documented both options and showed the lender why either would do. It could not tell the founders which risk they would rather live with.
